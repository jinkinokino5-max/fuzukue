The Railway at Matadi

崖を爆破していた鉄道

The Congo is one of the largest rivers in the world, but it has a problem. Near the sea, between Matadi and Stanley Pool, the river falls through a long series of rapids and waterfalls. Europeans called them the Livingstone Falls. No ship can pass them.

RECITE · I
Near the sea, between Matadi and Stanley Pool, the river falls through a long series of rapids and waterfalls. Europeans called them the Livingstone Falls. No ship can pass them.

So before the railway, everything that went up or down the river had to be carried around the rapids on foot. Ivory came down to the coast on the heads and shoulders of porters, and trade goods went up the same way. This is the road that Marlow will walk in the next chapters.

on foot 徒歩で・人の足でivory 象牙porter 運搬人trade goods 交易品

Henry Morton Stanley understood the problem very well. He is quoted as saying that without a railway, the Congo was not worth a penny.

be quoted as saying ~ 〜と言ったと伝えられるnot worth a penny 1ペニーの値打ちもない

A company was formed to build the line, and its director was Albert Thys, the same man who hired Conrad. Work began in 1890, the year Conrad arrived at Matadi. The route had to climb out of the deep river valley and cross the Crystal Mountains, so the first part of the work was slow and hard. The railway was finished only in 1898, eight years later. It was 366 kilometres long.

The cost in human lives was very high. According to the official count, 1,932 people died while building it: about 1,800 Africans and 132 Europeans. Historians believe that the real number was probably higher. Besides local people, workers were brought from far away, including Barbados and China.

Marlow does not tell us these numbers. He shows us a boiler lying in the grass, a truck on its back “as dead as the carcass of some animal,” and six men in chains carrying baskets of earth. A history book gives the facts; a novel makes us stand on the path and watch.
