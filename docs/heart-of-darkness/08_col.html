The Ivory Trade

In the nineteenth century, ivory was a little like plastic today. It was hard but easy to carve, it was smooth and white, and it did not break easily. Europeans and Americans used it for combs, knife handles, and many small objects in their homes.

Two products used more African ivory than any others: piano keys and billiard balls. Both were signs of a comfortable middle-class life. A family that played the piano in the evening, or a man who played billiards at his club, probably never thought about where the white material came from.

It came from elephants, and many of them. At the height of the trade, about 65,000 elephants were killed every year in tropical Africa. Guns made the killing much faster than before.

It came from elephants, and many of them. At the height of the trade, about 65,000 elephants were killed every year in tropical Africa.

Europeans did not usually hunt the elephants themselves. They bought the tusks from African hunters and traders, and paid with cheap goods: cotton cloth, beads, knives and brass rods. These are the “rubbishy cottons, beads, and brass-wire” that Marlow sees going “into the depths of darkness.” Then the tusks were carried to the coast by porters on foot, each man carrying a heavy tusk.

Some African artists carved scenes on tusks and sold them to European traders. On one tusk from Loango, near the mouth of the Congo, a long line of small figures climbs around the ivory in a spiral: traders, sailors, prisoners tied together with rope, and people bringing ivory for sale. The ivory itself, carried away to Europe, tells the story of the trade that took it there.

In Heart of Darkness, ivory is the only thing that really matters to the Company. The word “ivory” is everywhere in the story, and Marlow says the pilgrims whisper it as if they were praying to it. Keep this in mind when you meet Kurtz.
