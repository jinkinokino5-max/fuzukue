Before the railway was finished in 1898, the caravan road was the only way around the rapids of the lower Congo. Everything the Europeans needed upriver went along this road: food, cloth, tools, even the parts of steamboats. And everything they wanted from the interior, above all ivory, came back down the same way.

There were no horses or carts. All of it was carried by African porters, who walked in long lines with the loads on their heads. Marlow suggests why the villages along the road were empty: strangers with guns were catching people to carry loads.

Conrad arrived at Matadi on 13 June 1890. After about two weeks there, on 28 June, he set out on foot with a caravan of about thirty men. With him was a company agent named Prosper Harou.

Like Marlow’s companion, Harou fell ill on the way, with fever and a bilious attack. Near the end of July, he had to be carried in a hammock. Readers of the novel will remember the heavy man in the hammock, and the porters who ran away in the night.

Conrad reached Kinshasa, on Stanley Pool, on 1 August 1890. His diary is short and plain, with notes about distances, camps and the weather, and it stops soon after his arrival. Nine years later, these dry notes became some of the most vivid pages in English fiction.
