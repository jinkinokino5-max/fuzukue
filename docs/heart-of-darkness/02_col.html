Roman Britain

In 55 BC, Julius Caesar crossed the sea from Gaul to Britain. To the Romans, the island was the edge of the known world: cold, wet, and covered in mist. Caesar came back the next year with a bigger army and crossed the Thames, but he did not stay. He took some promises and some hostages, and sailed home.

Almost a hundred years later, in AD 43, the emperor Claudius sent four legions to Britain. This time the Romans came to stay. Their soldiers built roads, forts and harbours, and the south of the island slowly became a Roman province called Britannia.

A few years after the invasion, around AD 47, the Romans built a wooden bridge over the Thames. They chose a place where the river was narrow enough to bridge, but deep enough for sea-going ships. A town grew up beside the bridge, and they called it Londinium. This was the beginning of London, the “greatest town on earth” that Conrad describes in the first chapter.

Not everyone welcomed the newcomers. In AD 60 or 61, Boudica, a queen of the Iceni people, led a great revolt against Roman rule. Her army burned Londinium to the ground and killed many of the people living there. The Romans defeated her in the end, and they rebuilt the town.

The Roman historian Tacitus wrote about these wars. In his book Agricola, he gives a speech to Calgacus, a chief in the far north of Britain, who is about to fight the Romans. Calgacus calls the Romans “robbers of the world.” Then he says, “They make a desert and call it peace.” Historians think that Tacitus probably wrote this speech himself. Even so, it is a Roman writer who puts these hard words about Rome into an enemy’s mouth.

They make a desert and call it peace.

Remember what Marlow says about the Romans: “It was just robbery with violence, aggravated murder on a great scale.” Nearly two thousand years apart, the two voices sound very alike. Rome stayed in Britain for about four hundred years; by 410 its rule was over. Marlow’s point is simple and uncomfortable: the people who once came to Britain as conquerors were not so different from the Europeans who were now going to Africa.
